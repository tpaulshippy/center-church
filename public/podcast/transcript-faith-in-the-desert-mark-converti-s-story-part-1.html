Faith in the Desert: Mark Converti’s Story (Part 1)

Sep 10, 2025

Speaker A: This is where it starts now. Break on through.

Here we go.

Rich Richardson: Let it show.

Hey everybody, welcome back to Fixed on Jesus. This is the podcast that is hosted at Center Church in Gilbert, Arizona. Um, it's run by members of Center Church for members of Center Church. And my name is Rich, and as always, I'm joined by Mark Johnson Jr. just across the table.

Mark Johnson Jr.: Hello, Rich.

Rich Richardson: And Heather Corbett behind— not just behind the glass, but behind the camera now.

Mark Johnson Jr.: Oh, you saw the wave.

Rich Richardson: The wave is there. And we're actually joined today by a very special guest, Mark Converti. How you doing, Mark? Good to be with you. Glad to have you for sure. Our legion of fans are excited, uh, to be able to hear your story.

Mark Johnson Jr.: Yes.

Rich Richardson: And so, um, we, we wanted to invite you here to just tell— you're our longest-running church member, uh, having gotten to this church in 1978, you told me before. So before we get into that story, maybe Tell us about, uh, your family, your background, how you got saved.

Mark Converti: Well, um, I was born in the Valley. I was born actually in Mesa, um, and, uh, have lived my whole life in the Valley.

Rich Richardson: Did you grow up in the East Valley after you were born in Mesa?

Mark Converti: No, my, uh, my mother actually was a student at what is now Arizona State University. She had me on her graduation day.

Rich Richardson: Oh, wow.

Mark Converti: And, um, shortly thereafter we moved to North Phoenix, and that's where I grew up, actually, was in North Phoenix.

Rich Richardson: What passed for North Phoenix back then?

Mark Converti: Well, uh, our, our street was the northern limit of Phoenix at that time. That was Glendale Avenue.

Rich Richardson: That's not North Phoenix now.

Mark Converti: Glendale was a dirt road.

I remember clearly. And behind us, behind our neighborhood was desert.

So yeah, it's the middle of Phoenix now. So that was a long time ago.

Rich Richardson: Yeah. So you— but there's not many people who can say that they're Valley natives, but you are.

Mark Johnson Jr.: Yeah, I guess that's true. Yeah. Family-wise, my My father is— was born in Italy in 1925. He lasted through World War II, came to America shortly after.

I won't go into detail about his story, which is amazing, but he ended up coming as a student to the University of Arizona. He was a student? In Tucson.

Rich Richardson: So he went to Tucson?

Mark Converti: Yes.

Rich Richardson: Okay.

Mark Converti: And that's where he met my mother, who was living in Tucson with her parents. Her father was a professor at the U of A, and they ended up living next door to each other.

Rich Richardson: Really?

Mark Converti: Is how they met. And one thing led to another.

They got married. A few years later, my dad became a citizen of the United States. And we— he moved to Phoenix to work for Arizona Public Service, which is where he was for 38 years, I believe.

Rich Richardson: So APS, which is— which runs electricity on the west side?

Mark Converti: It's in central Phoenix. Mostly, and well, central Arizona mostly.

Rich Richardson: Okay.

Mark Converti: And it shares, uh, uh, the area with Salt River Project.

Rich Richardson: Okay. What was his— what did he study in school?

Mark Converti: Engineering. He was an electrical engineer, um, and that ended up being what I pursued as well years later. So, um, he, uh, he worked— that was his first job out of college. He worked his entire working life there and he retired after about 38 years. Wow.

Mark Johnson Jr.: Mark, I heard that, you know, just doing some research, that your dad helped build a lot of the modern-day power grid here through his work at APS. Is that fair to say?

Mark Converti: Yeah, if you drive up through, like from Phoenix up towards northern Arizona, you're going to pass underneath A lot of giant electrical wires that are strung among giant poles for miles and miles. That's the handiwork of his team. Wow.

Rich Richardson: Did they pull wires from Page?

Mark Converti: Yes, yes, because there's a generating station in the Page area. Also, there was one Up on the Navajo Indian Reservation, which has been deactivated recently.

Rich Richardson: Really?

Mark Converti: Yeah. Um, but anyway, that was his expertise, was electrical distribution. That's what he did.

Rich Richardson: Wow.

Mark Converti: Yeah.

Rich Richardson: Wow, that, that's amazing. Yeah. Now, uh, talk about like how many siblings do you have?

Mark Converti: Um, I am the oldest of 4.

I have 2 brothers and a sister.

Rich Richardson: Okay. Okay, and then what was the spiritual life like in your home, in the converting home? What was that like?

Mark Converti: Well, it was— I guess the best way to say it is it was dark.

My father was raised as a Catholic and— An Italian Catholic is different than like an American Catholic. Yes, it's hardcore. Let's put it that way.

Rich Richardson: Yeah.

Mark Converti: His teachers were Jesuits.

Rich Richardson: So Jesuit, what does that mean, Mark? Do you know what that word means? Do not look on the computer.

Mark Johnson Jr.: No, but I've heard conspiracy theories.

Rich Richardson: Jesus.

Mark Johnson Jr.: Yeah.

Mark Converti: Yes.

Rich Richardson: So it's the— it's the— it's an order. It's the order, uh, it's an order of, uh, like monk or Dominican. Like, so there's Dominicans, Augustinians, and the Jesuits were the hardcore— yeah, hardcore of the Catholic folks that came after the Reformation. So, right. They were part of the Counter-Reformation. So your dad grew up under the influence of the Jesuits.

Mark Converti: Yes. But I did mention that he met and married my mother.

Rich Richardson: Right.

Mark Converti: My mother was not a Catholic. She converted to Catholicism to get married to him. But about 5 years or so afterwards, she just couldn't take the Catholic Church.

Rich Richardson: Yeah.

Mark Converti: And as a couple, they decided to leave.

Rich Richardson: Really? and pursue other things spiritually. Wow.

Mark Converti: So by the time I was old enough to know anything, we were not really going to a specific church. As a matter of fact, it looked to me as if we were going— we were visiting every church, every kind of church there was in the valley, whether Christian or non-Christian.

Rich Richardson: Really? to just discover what they had to offer. And so that was kind of my spiritual upbringing. So they were really searching.

Mark Converti: They were really searching. And at a certain point, they dropped me and my brother off at a Congregationalist church in North Phoenix.

Rich Richardson: Did they let you vote as a kid?

No.

Mark Converti: But they let us learn the Bible, learn their process for what they called confirmation.

And so that would be the kind of spiritual upbringing I had through the end of high school.

Rich Richardson: But your parents weren't involved in that church?

Mark Converti: No, they weren't. They kept searching, and then they finally just stayed home. It looked like they gave up when it came to pursuing spiritual things. Yeah.

Rich Richardson: Okay.

Mark Johnson Jr.: Mark, can I ask, reflecting on that experience, do you think your parents were searching for the spiritual aspect or some of the social pressure in that day of wanting to be— like religion was viewed from what we understand as good. And were they trying to just be a part of the culture or were they genuinely concerned with the state of their soul?

Mark Converti: That's a very good question.

It seemed like a personal quest to me.

Mark Johnson Jr.: Yeah.

Mark Converti: That they were trying to find meaning.

You know, my father still had an education in religious things through his Catholic upbringing. And I think that that is something that he wanted to continue.

Mark Johnson Jr.: Yeah.

Mark Converti: My mother would have been more Protestant in her upbringing. But once again, I think they were looking for spiritual fulfillment.

Mark Johnson Jr.: Yeah.

Mark Converti: That they knew that there was something missing.

Mark Johnson Jr.: Yeah.

Mark Converti: And so, yeah.

Mark Johnson Jr.: Would they have— do you think they would have considered themselves Christians at that point?

Mark Converti: No, I don't think so.

Mark Johnson Jr.: They were a little uncertain.

Mark Converti: They were uncertain.

Mark Johnson Jr.: Okay. And we're going to get into your conversion.

Rich Richardson: Yeah. What happened? How did you get saved at the Congregational Church?

Mark Converti: No. Well, I don't think so.

I would say this, I felt religious.

Rich Richardson: Yeah.

Mark Converti: I felt like it was momentous.

It was significant.

I felt like when I went there, I was doing something good.

Rich Richardson: Yeah.

Mark Converti: Being amongst other people, saying that we were following God. But a number of things happened at that church That made me question whether or not it was really a valid or true form of religion.

Rich Richardson: Oh, really?

Mark Converti: Yeah. And it and this is this is a long story, but let me try to just well that's what we're here for.

Mark Johnson Jr.: We're here for it.

Mark Converti: Well, to to put it in a few words, the the when I was in high school and I belonged to their youth group.

Rich Richardson: Yeah.

Mark Converti: The youth pastor, pastors plural, were doing some very interesting things that I was wondering. They confused me, basically.

Rich Richardson: Were they handling snakes?

Mark Converti: No, they weren't doing that.

Rich Richardson: Okay, good. Well, that's that's that's good. They weren't doing that. But they were also searching through the through the the whole. world of religion to find meaning in different religions. Oh, wow.

Mark Converti: The Congregationalists are supposed to be a Protestant denomination. Yeah, right. They're supposed to be.

Rich Richardson: Very much.

Mark Converti: But I found out later that they tended towards modernism.

Rich Richardson: Okay.

Mark Converti: Which more or less devalues the divinity of Christ.

Rich Richardson: Right.

Mark Converti: But this youth, the youth pastor wanted to explore Eastern religions. Wow. And he wanted to explore spiritualism.

I remember very clearly once they invited an individual from a local group that was a clairvoyant.

Rich Richardson: Really? At a youth meeting?

Mark Converti: At a youth meeting.

Rich Richardson: Uh, we should make sure Frank's not doing that in our center. I actually don't know what a clairvoyant is.

Mark Converti: It's somebody who reads you.

Mark Johnson Jr.: Oh, uh, saying that he has some kind of spiritual insight. Okay.

Mark Converti: Into you as a person.

Mark Johnson Jr.: Okay. It's like a necromancer?

Rich Richardson: No, that's speaking to the dead.

Mark Converti: Yeah, yeah.

Rich Richardson: A clairvoyant, it's demonic all the same.

Mark Converti: Yeah, it's basically just trying to tell you Tell you who you are, kind of a prophetic utterance, if you will, to tell you who you are now, what you may be in the future.

Mark Johnson Jr.: From the wrong side. Well, there's like some some of them read palms, some of them use tarot cards. Okay.

Mark Converti: So this guy went around the room reading people.

Rich Richardson: Oh my God!

Mark Converti: And for some reason, he hopped over me, which he didn't read you. No, he didn't read me.

Rich Richardson: Okay.

Mark Johnson Jr.: God select.

Mark Converti: But he said interesting things to different people. And then he left. And that, of course, generated a lot of discussion.

Later, they did things like, you know, it was popular in that time for people to talk about Buddhism.

Rich Richardson: Yeah.

Mark Converti: And to interpret popular songs of the era.

Rich Richardson: Yeah.

Mark Converti: Like Simon and Garfunkel's Bridge Over Troubled Water, which is kind of a Yeah, a musical description of a passage in Buddhist scripture, as I understand it.

Rich Richardson: Yeah. And anyway, did they interpret Puff the Magic Dragon?

Mark Converti: No, not that one.

Rich Richardson: Okay, because we all know what that means, right, Mark?

Mark Johnson Jr.: Me?

Rich Richardson: Mark Johnson definitely knows what that means.

Mark Johnson Jr.: This sounds like those records that you talked about that you play backwards.

Rich Richardson: Mark knows all— Mark, I know we're going to get into music. No, we're not getting into Mac. Backmasking. Remember all that, Mark, in the '80s?

Mark Converti: Well, I mean, at the time we were in the '60s.

Rich Richardson: Yeah, right, but in the '80s— '70s. We did backmasking. Remember that?

Mark Converti: Right. Yeah, I remember backmasking. But anyway, you know, to just kind of wrap it up, the youth pastor would bring all these different influences in like we were on some kind of a spiritual quest, which just kind of added to the confusion.

Finally, he brought in a couple of born-again believers.

Rich Richardson: Finally!

Mark Converti: Yes, he did.

Which was a very interesting session because here are these 2 scrawny-looking guys dressed up in the, what I would call the Jesus freak uniform.

Rich Richardson: Yeah, what is that?

Mark Converti: Tell us. Levi's shirts, vests, and pants, of course. With little crosses everywhere.

Rich Richardson: Okay, on the vests?

Mark Converti: On the vests, yeah.

One of the guys brought in an enormous Bible and laid it on the lectern in front of us, and I don't remember too much of what he said except at the end he read Scripture, then he closed it up and he looked around the room and he said, basically, if you don't believe this, You're all going to hell.

And they left. And that, of course, disturbed everybody in the room.

Rich Richardson: Yeah.

Mark Converti: So much so that the senior pastor of the church asked to meet with the youth group the very next day, which was Sunday.

Rich Richardson: Really?

Mark Converti: After the normal meeting.

And he tried to explain what had just happened with these quote-unquote born-again people.

That they had— that he had, as a young man, grown up in what he called a fundamentalist Orthodox Christian faith. And it was only when he went to college and studied higher criticism— Yes. And some of the other stuff that he ran into at seminary, He went and got educated. That he began to realize that, uh, that things weren't quite the way that he thought they were. And then he described his own faith, which was basically modernistic.

Rich Richardson: Yeah, right. Let's push pause right there to just to explain some of the time frame for people, because the— like today, I think this is fair to say, Mark, you can tell me, but today born again is synonymous for Christian. It's one and the same. In that day, it was not. Like, you could be a good Christian person without talking about— at least culturally in the US— without talking anything about conversion or a change or trusting in Christ. You go to church and you're just assumed to be a Christian, right? Mm-hmm. And so, the born-again kind of identity comes during the Jesus Movement, right? And it's people who experience a new birth, And it has nothing to do with their ecclesiological understanding or whatever. They just experience Jesus, right?

And they're born again, right? That's what it was like?

Mark Converti: Yes, that's what these guys claimed to be. They claimed to be born-again Christians, which they had to explain to us meant that they believed in the inerrancy and plain language of Scripture, especially around the person of Jesus Christ. And that their belief was real, real simple. They preached the Gospel. They preached what they knew to be the Gospel.

Rich Richardson: Now, just real quick, higher criticism. Do you know what that is, Mark Johnson?

Mark Johnson Jr.: A little bit. We've asked you about it before, but it was— it's been a while.

Rich Richardson: Yeah. So higher criticism is— it was real popular at the end of the 19th century. The beginning of the 20th.

Mark Johnson Jr.: Spurgeon addressed it too.

Rich Richardson: Yeah, right.

Mark Johnson Jr.: That's where I read it.

Rich Richardson: So he— it's the idea that the Bible as we have it is not— definitely not inerrant. You— it's not all legitimate. It's not original. There's a lot of parts that are— that shouldn't be in there. And so there were scholars that would go through the scriptures, especially the New Testament, and say, this is probably not legitimate, you know, and take sections like that. And so there were whole fields of scholarly exploration that would basically undermine the scriptures. And this is well before all the manuscript evidence has been discovered since then. So like in the— when it flourished in the '20s, '30s, '40s, '50s, a lot of the manuscript evidence hadn't come to light yet.

Mark Johnson Jr.: Right.

Rich Richardson: Now we have a whole host of manuscript evidence that has come to light that shows the scriptures that we have, the 66 books of the, of the Bible, 37 old, 29 new, we see that what we have in the scriptures is what was written down originally. So even more confirmation, way more confirmation. But back then, that evidence didn't exist.

Mark Johnson Jr.: Yeah.

Rich Richardson: And so people threw shade on it, which is what Mark's— his pastor at the time— well, it was popular in a lot of churches to deny. So like that, they They might— I don't know if this is the way it was there, but they would deny the resurrection of Christ. They would say, yeah, he died, but as an example. Yeah. It was not a— there was nothing about the power of gospel change. It was really, let's be good people and follow the sacrificial example of Jesus. Was that what it was like?

Mark Converti: The way that the pastor put it was that he didn't believe that Jesus was active in the believer's life after he came the first time.

Rich Richardson: Yeah, right.

Mark Converti: Okay, that when he would come again, there would be a second coming. But in between that period of when he died and rose to heaven and he came back, he wasn't really involved in the day-to-day life of Christians or anybody else.

Rich Richardson: Right.

Mark Converti: He called it like winding up a clock and letting it run.

Rich Richardson: Deism is what that's called, friends.

Mark Johnson Jr.: Okay.

Rich Richardson: Very popular in the past. Yes, go ahead.

Mark Johnson Jr.: So your 2 on-fire Jesus freak Christians came in and told you the truth, and then the pastor who had poor theology came back around and tried to correct them.

Mark Converti: Tried to bring everybody back down.

Rich Richardson: Did it calm everybody down?

Mark Converti: I think it did briefly, but I think people like me, there was a big question mark from that point on. And a few other things happened that year at that church, which was my senior year in high school, that led me to believe that I really wasn't aligned personally with what was going on. One was a trip that the youth group took up north, which included not just the people that were going to the church at that time, but also a bunch of my friends from my high school.

Rich Richardson: Oh, yeah?

Mark Converti: Central High in Phoenix.

Rich Richardson: Okay.

Mark Converti: I found out when we arrived that people had been planning ahead for this trip.

Rich Richardson: Okay.

Mark Converti: By buying large quantities of— Praying.

Rich Richardson: Praying. Oh, okay, not prayer and notebooks and making sure everybody had Bibles.

Mark Converti: Yeah, as soon— They brought drugs?

Mark Johnson Jr.: Not communion alcohol.

Mark Converti: Yeah, within a few hours of showing up at where we ended up meeting, which was another church in Flagstaff, my fellow students began breaking out lots of interesting drugs that they brought with them. Popular during the day were like LSD, Oh, wow. And marijuana, not so much cocaine and the others, but basically lots of pills. And one by one, these people started to take them and go off on little trips.

Rich Richardson: I bet they did. Yeah.

Mark Converti: Yeah. And they didn't go just to Flagstaff, did they?

Mark Johnson Jr.: No. Different kinds of trips.

Mark Converti: They tried to climb the foothills around the mountains in Flagstaff. While under the influence. And, you know, I was fairly innocent about all this kind of stuff. I didn't take drugs. I didn't hang around with people that did. And I was very shocked to see that my fellow youth church members were partaking as liberally as they were with all this stuff.

Rich Richardson: Gotcha.

Mark Converti: When the trip was over, The entire youth staff was fired.

Rich Richardson: Good.

Mark Converti: But at that point, I decided, you know, at the end of high school, I'm leaving my high school and I'm also leaving this church because there's nothing here I want. There's nothing here that I would want to emulate. And so that kind of marked the beginning of my Spiritual journey.

Mark Johnson Jr.: It sounds like some common grace on your life. You, at that point, you weren't a Christian, but you still knew what the wrong way was, and it was staying where you were at. Mark was not Christian, but he was against acid trips. I was not.

Mark Converti: Not you.

Mark Johnson Jr.: I'm talking to this Mark. Oh, this Mark? Oh, okay. You're looking at me. I went the other way. Anyways.

Mark Converti: Yeah, I didn't smoke, didn't really drink, and I definitely didn't take drugs, and so I had very little in common with these people. And so I went off to college, basically.

Rich Richardson: To ASU, right?

Mark Converti: First to Phoenix College for a couple of years and then eventually to ASU. And that's kind of where you could say I began to notice the absence, of anything real, really spiritual in my life.

Rich Richardson: Okay.

Mark Converti: It was kind of a gulf inside of me that kept getting bigger and bigger all the time. And I met a lot of interesting people. I was preached to half a dozen times by the Christians of the day, which were mostly Jesus people.

Rich Richardson: With their uniforms.

Mark Converti: With their uniforms.

Rich Richardson: Long hair and beards, right?

Mark Converti: Yep. Or other things. And I was, in fact, I was preached to at both Phoenix College and ASU by, I think it was, could be Campus Crusade at that time. Yeah, sure. But it didn't register at that moment. It took until the end of my first semester at ASU when I just realized, you know, I was in kind of a desperate place. I don't know whether it was because I wasn't doing well with my studies or I was just increasingly anxious about not having a purpose.

Rich Richardson: Yeah.

Mark Converti: In my life. And at that point, I think That's when God began to put things in my path. I saw a book lying on my best friend's dorm room table one day. It was called Satan Is Alive and Well on Planet Earth, which was a book, a follow-up book— That's a title right there. By Hal Lindsey.

Rich Richardson: Hal Lindsey, yeah.

Mark Converti: Who had written Late, Great Planet Earth.

Rich Richardson: Have you heard of Hal Lindsey, Mark?

Mark Johnson Jr.: No.

Rich Richardson: Heather had? No? Late Great— it was a phenomenon, especially Late Great Planet Earth.

Mark Converti: Yeah. Well, this book in particular, I took it kind of home to me, to my dorm room, and read it until midnight that night. Read it cover to cover. And at the end of the book, which was basically telling story after story about people who thought they were believers.

Rich Richardson: Yeah.

Mark Converti: But instead followed the devil, if you will, and into the occult and all kinds of different things, to the point where, you know, Hal came along, and in his case, and helped people see the light and see that they've been following the devil. At the end of this book, I think it was chapter 13, of all things. Of course, yeah. Is a prayer. And the prayer was basically a prayer of confession of sin and asking Jesus to come into your heart and save you.

Rich Richardson: Excellent.

Mark Converti: And so I prayed that prayer, and I remember feeling as though a weight had lifted off of me.

Rich Richardson: Really?

Mark Converti: Yeah. And I didn't know what that meant, to be honest. I knew that something had happened, but to be honest, for the next 4 years that it took for me to complete my education, I did not really pursue other Christians. I didn't pursue going to church. I didn't read the Bible. I didn't do any of those spiritual things.

Rich Richardson: Sure.

Mark Converti: That you really should do if you are a true believer. And so it wasn't until 4 years later, which is now the summer of 1978, that once again, God began to do some things in my life.

And in particular, I was, at the same time I was studying engineering, I was also part of the ASU Symphony Orchestra playing my cello, which is something I'd done since grade school. It wasn't something I was doing for credit either. It was— music was just kind of a release.

Rich Richardson: Yeah.

Mark Converti: A hobby. It was like a way to satisfy myself emotionally.

Rich Richardson: Sure, yeah.

Mark Converti: And what I didn't know is that there were people in that orchestra that were saved and that had noticed me and were praying for me.

Rich Richardson: Oh, wow.

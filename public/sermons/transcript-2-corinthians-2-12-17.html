Now what exactly is a triumphal procession? It's not a term we use these days. but it was something that happened back in those days. Back in the Roman times. Essentially it's a parade of sorts. But it's a special one. Back in Paul's time when a commanding Roman general returned from a successful military conquest he was often honored with a triumphal procession through the streets of Rome.

We have similar types of parades today.

When we honor the reigning Super Bowl or World Series champions such extravaganzas typically consist of the winning team parading through the home city to the delight of their fans.

In such parades the champions are not only the center of attention they're the only ones participating in it. No one else except a few family and friends ride through the streets with the champions. But interestingly in Roman triumphs the parade included many many more participants not just the triumphant general.

Let's hear how a man named H.S. Versinal described them in his 400 page dissertation on Roman triumphal processions that he submitted for his doctoral thesis.

A Roman triumph had a fairly standard processional order.

First came the captives usually walking in chains and often destined for execution or further display.

Next in line all on foot came Rome's senators and magistrates then the general and his four horse chariot his unarmed soldiers and freed prisoners of war followed in togas and laurel crowns. All this was done to the accompaniment of music the strewing of flowers and clouds of incense.

Now that's a lot of participants for a victory parade.

Up front were the captives the losers of war destined to die in disgrace.

In back were those freed by the general and the soldiers returning home to their families and dispersed throughout almost invisible to the onlookers were those who played music and threw flowers and burned incense.

But the clear centerpiece of it all remained the one elevated on the four horse chariot. The triumphant one himself riding as it were through a cloud of incense.

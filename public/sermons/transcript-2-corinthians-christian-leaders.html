Coincidentally, it's right after 1 Corinthians and right before Galatians.

We'll be in 2 Corinthians chapter 1, verses 23 through chapter 2, verse 4. And in that section, we're going to discover the answer to this question. What is Christian leadership? What is Christian leadership? Is Christian leadership just leaders who happen to be Christians or Christians who happen to be leaders? What about leaders in the church? Should leaders in the church look differently than the leaders in our culture? If so, how? There's a whole cluster of questions that we're going to see all the way through 2 Corinthians that speaks directly to leadership in the church. One of the things that we've talked about and will continue to talk about in 2 Corinthians is that 2 Corinthians presents the paradox of Christian living in a fallen world.

It might surprise you, but the Bible has much to say on Christian leadership in the church and 2 Corinthians in particular. Joel Bells, the late founder of World Magazine, wrote the following about leadership in a column nearly 20 years ago. He's speaking about national government, but his thoughts apply equally to church leadership. He says, The sentence still jumps out at me from the middle of an editorial in the Wall Street Journal. It's been a decade since I read it, but it was one of those electric expressions that you can't forget. People want to be lightly governed, the writer said, by strong governments. I'll read it again. People want to be lightly governed, the writer said, by strong governments.

I might adapt it to say, we want our Christian leaders to be strong, but kind and gentle toward us. We want our pastors to be strong, but kind and gentle toward us. We want our leaders to be resilient and caring. We want our pastors to be capable and kind. We want our leaders to be firm, but understanding. We want our pastors to be protective, yet selfless. We want our leaders to be decisive, but listen. We want our leaders to be strong, but gentle.

In short, we want our pastors and leaders to reflect Jesus Christ.

Now, no leader on earth will be a perfect reflection of Jesus. But every faithful Christian leader, every faithful Christian pastor must resemble Jesus to some degree in the way they lead. faithful Christian leaders show their authority through service as they lead. And inevitably, Marvin would be caught, tackled, and everyone would jump on the pile, thus dogpile. And poor Marvin was left at the bottom of the dogpile, pondering his life choices.

But there was one benefit, because Marvin had the authority to call out the next victim. He could stand up and authoritatively respond by saying, dogpile on Michael. And Michael was in trouble. And on and on it went. You see, when there's a church challenge, oftentimes, what happens is, the dispute gets so rancorous that people lose perspective and start dogpiling on each other. And the church at this point in Corinth was dogpiling on Paul, piling insult upon insult on Paul. And the irony is, they responded to Paul by correcting the sexually immoral man. But they appeared not to like the way that Paul spoke to them. So they piled on more and more and more and more. And in fact, if you read 2 Corinthians, you can find a smattering of insults that Paul repeats that they threw his way.

Here's a few examples. They called him fickle. 2 Corinthians 1.17 says, was I vacillating when I wanted to do this? Do I make my plans according to the flesh? Saying, this is what they said to him, yes, yes, and no, no at the same time? That's what they said about him. They called him puny. In chapter 10, verse 10, for they say, his letters are weighty and strong, but his bodily presence is weak and his speech is of no account. He's a keyboard warrior, we might say today, they were saying.

Again, his letters were harsh, but his presence was weak. 2 Corinthians 10, verse 1. I, Paul, he says, myself entreat you by the meekness and gentleness of Christ. And here he repeats their challenge or their insult. I who am humble when face to face with you, but bold toward you when I am away. They criticized his speaking ability. Even if I am unskilled in speaking, Paul says in chapter 11, I am not so in knowledge. Indeed, in every way, we have made this plain to you in all things.

See, this is what happens when churches get into trouble. They start dogpiling on each other, oftentimes, first the leaders and then everybody else. No one might have understood why it started, but it just continues. And continues and continues and continues. And everybody was expressing their negative opinions about Paul. But notice how Paul defends himself. He does not say, how dare you? I'm the apostle to the Gentiles. Nor does he say, who do you think you are? Do you know that I'm writing most of the New Testament?

Nor does he say, nobody knows you, but history is going to remember me.

Rather, look at what he says. Here's why. Here's how he defended himself. Verse 23. But I call God to witness against me. It was to spare you that I refrained from coming again to Corinth.

Not that we lord it over your faith, but we work with you for your joy. For you stand firm in your faith. Paul was working for their good and he was working to maximize their joy. He's not trying to lord his authority over them. He's doing all that he could so that they might have more joy in the Lord. He decided that if he were to come to them and they were loggerheads, they would be in no position to come to an understanding of harmony.

Sometimes, confrontation is not, it's not the best, it's not always the best time always to confront and this is what Paul saw. He had every right to come and to confront them and to correct them for the way they were harboring this sexually immoral man. He could have come with a sword and eviscerated them. Instead, he wrote a letter. Why? To spare them. Why? For their joy.

See, Christian leaders serve, they live and their purpose is to lead and serve in such a way so that the people they lead have greater joy in Christ. And Paul knew that if he visited them, it might put an immovable wedge between him and the congregation. And if there's an immovable wedge between him and them, that wedge eventually might be a wedge between the congregation and the Lord.

And so what Paul did to maximize their joy was giving them space. He didn't drop by.

He wrote a letter so that they would receive it better.

Christian leaders need to know their flock well enough to know what will maximize the joy of the people that they lead.

Now I can tell you that when the Corinthians read this letter and when they recognized that he did not come, they weren't thinking that he was working for their joy.

But that's exactly what he was doing.

Oftentimes, Christian leaders, faithful leaders, will do things to maximize the joy of the people they lead. It just won't feel that way.

Sometimes leaders must challenge the hottie, encourage the weak, teach against the grain, call people to repentance. Sometimes they give people space. Sometimes they express grace. Sometimes they take a step back. Sometimes they gently point people in another direction. But all the time, they're called to be motivated for the good, not just the good, but for the joy of the people that they lead. Christian leaders must strive to maximize the joy of the people they lead. So how do they work for the good of others? Working to maximize the joy in other people and the people that they lead. Secondly, Christian leaders serve by doing what is best for others.

And this is often, this often means that they don't do what's best for themselves.

Paul had their best in mind when he decided not to drop by. He wanted what was best for them even though they did not see it that way. And he explains himself in verse 1. For I made up my mind not to make another painful visit to you. See, he had already done this before. He came, they didn't listen. It was painful. He wrote a letter. They finally listened.

And he knew that if he came back, if he came back, it would be it would be a boxing match. They would be sparring.

And that's not what he wanted. That's what he says in verse 2. For if I cause you pain, who is there to make me glad but the one whom I pain? In other words, if you're having trouble with me and I'm having trouble with you, how are we going to mutually benefit each other? How are you going to make me glad and how am I going to make you glad? See, ideally a relationship between a leader and a church is mutually beneficial. If Paul was sad, they can encourage him. If they were down, Paul can encourage them. But when they're at odds and they're in tension, he judged it better not to come so that they could have space to process his correction. Sometimes, a strategic retreat in a dispute is the best thing we can do.

And he reiterates that idea in verse 3. And I wrote as I did so that when I came, I might not suffer pain from those who should have me rejoice. For I felt sure of all of you that my joy would be the joy of you all. In other words, what good does it do for us to be together if we're going to be at each other's throats?

Sometimes, the best thing we can do is gather our wits, remember what's priority, what's most important, rehearse why we appreciate that other person, then get together in time. See, Christian leaders must work for the good of the people that they serve. And this means Christian leaders must know their flock well enough to know what is best. Paul knew it would not be best for him to press them hard so as to exasperate them.

Paul's goal was not just to be right or to be seen to be right. He wanted what was best for the dear saints in Corinth. He wanted them not to sinfully judge and marginalize him.

He didn't want to be misunderstood anymore. See, this is often one of the hardest things in Christian leadership. There's no manual to refer to. There's no manual that says, when you're in a dispute with someone or when you're in a dispute with a group of people, here's what you do. Say this, don't say this. Do this, don't do that. But that's not true. We just have to use wisdom.

And so, faithful Christian leaders will do what's best for the other people. And oftentimes that means they're doing not what's convenient.

They're not doing what's easy. But they're doing what's best.

Paul wasn't communicating, this is easier for me not to come. In fact, we'll see in verse 4 that he was bothered to the depth of his soul.

See, sometimes as leaders it's best to speak clearly, other times it's better to hold your tongue.

Sometimes it's best to press for change, and other times it's best to pull back and give space. Sometimes it's best to express truth strongly, and other times it's better to rehearse grace loudly. Sometimes it's better to listen carefully, other times it's better to press ahead. And a skilled faithful leader will know what to do, not because he's smart, or because he's more intelligent than everyone, but because he is guided by what is best for those that he serves. Because Christian leadership is not about self-fulfillment.

If you want to be fulfilled, don't become a Christian leader. If you want to be challenged, if you want to suffer, if you want to work hard when no one seems to understand or get it, then become a Christian leader. Biblical leaders follow the example of Jesus. He actively laid down his life. He laid down his preferences. He laid down his rights. He laid down his privileges for the good of others. Faithful Christian leaders are people who are called to follow in his steps, laying down their lives and their preferences and their rights and their privileges for the good of other people.

It's easy as a Christian leader to live in such a way as to maximize personal convenience, to avoid hard conversations, or to avoid taking unpopular stands, or to avoid speaking clearly, or to avoid confronting influential people, or to duck hard situations. Sometimes faithful leaders must have the hard conversation, take the unpopular stand, speak clearly, confront the influential, and wade into difficult situations.

Faithful leaders run to the places where there are problems. Faithful leaders don't look and say, man, I don't want to get involved in that.

You might say that, but they go and get involved anyway.

I've talked to so many different faithful Christian leaders over the years and heard their stories of hardship and trial and asked, what did you feel when you saw that situation? Surely you didn't want to go and get involved. And they say, absolutely I didn't, but that was the right thing to do. A Christian leader, a faithful Christian leader, serves the good of others and not for the convenience of himself. And this becomes incredibly hard when a leader is misunderstood, maligned, or lied about. It would be easy and natural even for him to go around correcting everyone to set the record straight. It's not what Paul does here.

Why? Because the Christian leader is meant to make much of Christ and not of himself. More important than anything is not, more important than anything is what a church thinks about Jesus and not her pastor. Christian leaders are to lead for the good of other people. Christian leaders say with their actions, forget me, focus on Jesus. Ignore me, but listen to Jesus. Marginalize me, but sacrifice for the Lord. Disregard me, but lead, press into Jesus. Why? Because faithful leaders understand the best thing for a church is to know and follow hard after Jesus. First, last, and most important.

Acts 9:20-31

Rich Richardson · Acts · May 24, 2026

Meeting the King transforms everything.

chapter 9, verses 20 to 31. Make your way there. We'll be looking at the second part of Saul's conversion. You may have heard the phrase before, April showers bring May flowers. Well, if you're from the Midwest, at least. Arizonians, maybe not. But this time of year, in the Midwest, flowers are blooming all across the fields. But they happen because of rain, endless rain, that comes in April. When that rain comes, it nourishes the ground that leads to a full spring bloom, beautiful, beautiful flowers, vibrant green grass, and trees that are just, you know, just dotting the landscape with all sorts of colors. My wife and I just returned from a trip from the Midwest, and we had a really unfortunate timing because we did all the April showers part, but we missed the May flowers.

This catchy rhyme describes for us a certain cause and effect relationship that happens every spring. Rain leads to life. It's an inevitable phenomenon that we Arizonians don't get to experience much. There are lots of cause and effect relationships in our world. Certain inevitabilities that arise when an event occurs. For example, when it's 110 degrees outside, your dashboard is hot. When you jump into a pool, you get wet. When you walk into a Starbucks, you drink bad coffee. And so on, and so on. Where we find ourselves today in Acts is within a certain cause and effect relationship.

Last week, we witnessed the conversion of Saul, the chief persecutor of the Christian church, who is appointed by Jesus to become the one to bring the gospel to all peoples. Our passage today explores the immediate aftermath of his conversion. We get to see the immediate effects that happen in Saul's life and in the life of the church as a result of God coming to Saul and meeting with him. If we were to put a clever rhyme on this cause and effect relationship, something like April showers bring May flowers would be summarized like this. Meeting the king transforms everything. Say it again. Meeting the king transforms everything. What we are going to see in our passage is what happens when someone, anyone, truly meets Jesus Christ, the son of God. It can be described in one word, transformation. All of us come into this room this morning in need of some kind of transformation.

Maybe it's an internal transformation. You're aware of your consistent lack of satisfaction with life, or you have a sin struggle that only seems to be getting worse. Your outlook on life is filled with hopelessness and despair, or you feel like you have no meaning or purpose. Or maybe it's an external transformation. There's a relationship that you have that is in need of restoration, whether it be with a spouse, with a parent, or a child, a fellow believer, a co-worker, a brother, a brother, sister, relative, or neighbor. Or maybe there's a particularly difficult circumstance you're in right now. Unemployment, financial trouble, physical ailment, illness, suffering, or even persecution. Or maybe, like Saul, prior to his conversion experience on the Damascus Road, you're unaware of any need for things to change at all. Wherever you may be, all of us need transformation. Our text this morning reveals what happens when someone really meets Jesus, that they are both internally renewed and externally reoriented, leading to a strengthening and expanding of God's people throughout the world.

Would you join me as I read Acts chapter 9? I'm going to start in the second half of verse 19, and we'll go down to verse 31. Word of God reads, For some days he was with the disciples at Damascus, and immediately he proclaimed Jesus in the synagogue, saying, He is the Son of God. And all who heard him were amazed and said, Is not this the man who made havoc in Jerusalem of all of those who called upon this name? And has he not come here for this purpose, to bring them bound before the chief priests? But Saul increased all the more in strength and confounded the Jews who lived in Damascus by proving that Jesus was the Christ. When many days had passed, the Jews plotted to kill him. But their plot became known to Saul. They were watching the gates day and night in order to kill him.

But his disciples took him by night and let him down through an opening in the wall, lowering him in a basket. And when he had come to Jerusalem, he attempted to join the disciples. And they were all afraid of him, for they did not believe that he was a disciple. But Barnabas took him and brought him to the apostles and declared to them how on the road he had seen the Lord, who spoke to him, and how at Damascus he had preached boldly in the name of Jesus. So he went in and out among them at Jerusalem, preaching boldly in the name of the Lord. And he spoke and disputed against the Hellenists. But they were seeking to kill him. When the brothers learned this, they brought him down to Caesarea and sent him off to Tarsus. And the church throughout all Judea and Galilee and Samaria had peace.

It was being built up and walking in the fear of the Lord and in the comfort of the Holy Spirit. It multiplied. Let's pray. Lord, may your word not return void this morning. We ask that by the power of the Spirit, you would awaken our hearts to what you are transforming in us. Whether it be faith itself, whether it be the deliverance from sin or from a circumstance. God, we ask that you would lead us to you. In Jesus' name we pray. Amen.

We're going to observe a transformation in three points this morning. First is an internal transformation. Our story picks up after Saul's conversion with him joining the community of believers in Damascus. And immediately, Saul is in the synagogues proclaiming that Jesus is the Son of God, much to the confusion of the onlookers. The man who only days or maybe even hours earlier was planning to invade these synagogues and arrest Christians is now in the synagogues proclaiming Jesus. How perplexing. Imagine how perplexing it would be for us in modern day to see U.S. Senator Elizabeth Warren at a campaign rally talk about her love for Donald Trump. Or on a less political note, imagine listening to the press conference of the Arizona Diamondbacks manager and he's wearing a Dodgers jersey. It just wouldn't make any sense.

That's a fraction of the confusion that these Jewish people must have been thinking when out of Saul's mouth came arguments for Jesus being the Son of God. Wasn't this guy supposed to hate Christians? Everyone knew what Saul was about. Earlier in the chapter, Saul is described as breathing threats against Christians. He made no attempt to hide his intentions. He hated them. Then he does what no one expects. He preaches Jesus in the very place he intended to invade. And he does so immediately.

Paul didn't take Paul, Paul and Saul. Paul is the Greek or the Roman name for Saul. Saul is the Jewish name. Saul did not take time to learn all the Christianese. He didn't lay low and wait to get to a place where no one knew who he was and he could kind of rebuild his reputation as a Christ follower. No. At once, he's proclaiming the good news about his risen Lord to the people he intended to destroy. Why? Because Saul was transformed. A man filled with rage and bent on wiping Christianity off the face of the earth Saul is now boldly preaching the Son of God because he has tasted and seen the all-satisfying grace of his Savior and received a new heart.

Saul proclaims Jesus because his heart has been transformed. In Luke's gospel, Jesus is recorded saying, Do you ever want to know what's going on in someone's heart? Listen closely to what they talk about and how. Do you ever want to know what someone loves? Listen closely to what they talk about most and get most passionate about. Do you ever want to know what really lies at the core of someone's heart? Observe them in a situation that forces them to react emotionally, whether it be due to stress, pressure, tiredness, or frustration, and listen to what comes out of their mouth. Out of the overflow of the heart, the mouth speaks. Our hearts are like a reservoir, and our mouths are like a faucet. We put a little pressure on the reservoir and out spews from our mouths whatever is inside.

What do your words reveal about what you love most? What do your words reveal about what's going on internally? As a new dad, not a new dad, but a dad of a relative newborn, sleep has been one of the things that doesn't happen a whole lot. And it wasn't until we had a child that I learned quite how angry I was when I was tired at two in the morning and my baby wasn't falling asleep. All of us have something in our heart that comes out when there's pressure, or there's frustration, or there's tiredness. Everyone knew well what Saul loved. It was his Jewish tradition. And what was undermining that Jewish tradition was Christianity. So he was so singularly focused on that mission of destroying Christianity that everyone knew. He probably talked about it all the time.

He's described as breathing threats. Then he meets Jesus. A heart completely opposed to Jesus is now transformed to be for Jesus. A heart that delighted in nothing more than seeing Christians arrested, tried, imprisoned, and even killed, now delighted in the sweet, all-satisfying love and grace that comes from his Savior, Jesus Christ. A heart that fundamentally cared about self, culture, and tradition, now cared about Jesus, his mission, and his people. Meeting the King transforms everything. When someone comes face to face with their Maker, Jesus Christ, an internal renewal begins. For when you truly meet Jesus and experience his goodness, your heart of stone is stripped away and replaced with a heart of flesh. A heart that beats for the glory of God and for the good of his people. Do you want to know what your heart loves most?

Examine your words. What do you talk about most? What do you get fired up about? Is it your kids? Your favorite sports team? Your politics? Your culture? Yourself? Your problems? Other people's problems?

Your job? The news? Each one of us is devoted to something. It's in our nature to love things, to idolize things, to care about things. And if you listen long enough, closely enough, you begin to see what your heart really loves. It's not just about what we love. Our mouths can reveal what sin dwells deep inside. Bitterness towards a specific person manifested in being curt, dismissive, or rude. Rage towards someone who wrongs you. Annoyance over something that inconveniences you.

Jealousy of what someone else has. Demands for lustful desires to be fulfilled. Gossip to mask insecurity. Flattery in the form of people pleasing. Lying for personal gain. Our mouths are a window to our hearts. What lies inside eventually comes out. So we must all examine our hearts and ask to meet Jesus. Because when we meet Jesus, our hearts are transformed from loving the world to loving him. They are cleansed from all unrighteousness by the power of his blood.

And they are renewed and redeemed. Does your tongue reveal a need for transformation this morning? Meet with Jesus. He can renew what's broken inside. Does your heart need cleansing from a hidden sin? Meet with Jesus. His blood has the power to wipe away every stain. Meeting the King transforms everything. What begins internally, then moves externally. And that brings us to our second point.

An external transformation. After Saul's preaching in the Damascan synagogues, many days pass and the Jews plot to kill him. What's not immediately clear is how many days have passed. But Luke gives us a clue in Galatians, or Saul, Paul gives us a clue in Galatians 1, 15 through 18. It says this, But when he, that's Jesus, or God and Jesus, had set me apart before I was born, and who called me by his grace, was pleased to reveal his son to me, in order that I might preach among the Gentiles, I did not immediately consult with anyone, nor did I go up to Jerusalem to those who were apostles before me. But I went away into Arabia and returned again to Damascus. And after three years, I went up to Jerusalem to visit Cephas, and remained with him 15 days. So after Saul's early stint in Damascus, potentially three years pass before he arrives back on the scene, only to be met with a plot to kill him by the Jews.

When he learns of this plot, with the help of his disciples, he escapes Damascus and heads off to Jerusalem. Here we see the first fulfillment of Jesus' promise that he made in verse 16 to Ananias. He said, For I, Jesus, will show him, Saul, how much he must suffer for the sake of my name. Saul, the man who was once the hunter, has become the hunted. But when Saul was met with his opposition, he wasn't left to face it alone. For during this roughly three-year period, Saul surely spent most of that time with fellow believers. And over time, as it became clear that God was doing something extraordinary through him, others joined up with him as his followers or disciples. Like the Damascan Christians who received God's divine protection when Jesus appeared to Saul on the road to Damascus, Saul now receives divine protection as well in his escape out of Damascus.

But this time, it's protection provided by God through the people of God. In becoming a believer, Saul became a member of a community. Not just any community. The community of God. The church. As we have seen through Acts, those who belong to the church have their needs met. Saul receives his first taste of God's protection for him when his disciples help him escape. It's a good reminder for us that God has many means at his disposal in which to care for his people. Sometimes it's direct divine intervention, like when he protected the Damascan Christians from Saul in revealing himself to Saul on the road to Damascus. But other times, and more often, it's through more ordinary means, like his church.

When you are saved, your community is transformed. Saul was a part of a community before. The Pharisees. But when he meets Jesus, his former community rejects him. They turn against him. But because Saul has rooted himself to a local body of believers who he shared life with, when his moment of need came, the church was ready to help. The church continues to operate this way. Jesus delights to meet your needs through his people. But in order for this to happen, you need to be meaningfully connected to community. I say meaningfully because American Christianity broadly has reduced church to an hour and a half Sunday morning production that we consume and then are off on our merry way.

Do we think that Saul and his disciples just gathered once a week for an hour and a half to sing and hear God's word and then spend the rest of their week somewhere else? No. If they followed the pattern of churches formed in Acts, they held all things in common, spent regular time doing life together, serving one another, learning about each other, and talking about Jesus and all he had done and was doing for them. And so it must be for us. We must make the time to do life together as a local body of believers. Whether it be through attending a small group or caring for a homebound saint or serving in the youth or children's ministry or breaking bread together or evangelizing together, we must make every effort to know and care for one another. And we don't just do this so that when our hour of need comes, there are people ready to help.

But we also do this so that when the needs of others come about, we are ready to help. This is what Christian community looks like. But it's not the only mark of Christian community we see here in Acts chapter 9. From Damascus, Saul heads off to Jerusalem. If you recall, about three years before, he left Jerusalem to go to Damascus. At that point in time, he was known as the chief persecutor of the Christians. Now he's returning as a born-again believer. And he intends to join the Christian community there. Well, he hits a bit of a snag. He still has the reputation of being a Christian killer.

These Christians are afraid of him. And rightfully so. Though it's been three years since his conversion, no one in Jerusalem has seen the converted Saul. There may have been reports that made their way from Damascus to these Christians, but these people knew who Saul used to be. They saw him applaud the stoning of Stephen to death. They saw him ravage gatherings and have countless Christians arrested. How could these Christians trust him? How could they welcome a man who did such damage into their presence? Was this all a coup? Was Saul faking his conversion to get at the very heart of the Christian movement and stamp it out forever?

If we were these Christians, I'm quite confident we'd be thinking some of these things. We'd rather keep him at a distance. I'd probably say something like, hey, praise God if he's been converted, but we want nothing to do with him here. Send him off to Tucson. But that's not quite how God's community works. One man, Barnabas, whose name means son of encouragement, extends a hand of welcome. The text says that Barnabas took him and brought him to the apostles and declared to them how on the road he had seen the Lord who spoke to him and how at Damascus he had preached boldly in the name of Jesus. Notice where all the action rests. Rests on Barnabas. Barnabas takes Saul.

Barnabas gets to know Saul. Barnabas brings him before the apostles. Barnabas is the one who declares what happened in Saul's life. Barnabas was bold in initiating a relationship with Saul. He saw an outsider and moved towards him. and he got to know him. He heard Saul's story and witnessed genuineness in his faith. In this case, Barnabas acted as the bridge that brought the newly converted Saul together with the apostles and believers in Jerusalem. Barnabas' boldness led to Saul being welcomed and it's, you know, quite ironic that Barnabas' name means son of encouragement. What an example Barnabas is of what it looks like as a transformed believer to see outsiders? In what other community would a man or woman welcome someone in after seeing this person bring such destruction on a community?

This is completely counter-cultural. Our culture says today to cancel or cut people out of your life who disagree with you or don't affirm every quality about you let alone those who actually have done harm to you. This is not so with the church. Church, Barnabas serves as an example for us of how a transformed believer welcomes outsiders into community. Barnabas wasn't playing ignorant. He knew who Saul used to be but he trusted that God's saving power was strong enough to save someone even like Saul. What a display of the power of the gospel that even former enemies can come together through reconciliation. It begins by someone taking a step towards another. Has someone in the church mistreated you? Think of Jesus who was mistreated for the sins of mankind yet underwent such mistreatment motivated by his great love for you and me.

Has someone taken advantage of you? Think of how our Lord was taken advantage of at the cross and gave himself up for us. Has someone broken your trust? Contemplate the unswerving trust that Jesus had in the Father's plan to redeem us. Regardless of the sin that was committed against him he was resolute to see the plan through. We all at one point were outsiders. We were outside the family of God and we needed someone to welcome us in. Jesus did this for us. How then can we not do that for others? I can attest to the fact that this church is a welcoming church.

Seven years ago when Alexis and I were looking for a church in the East Valley Center Church stood out for its unique hospitality. I think the first six weeks we attended a different couple came up to us introduced themselves and invited us over for a meal. It was unlike any other experience that we had. Church let us not stop welcoming outsiders into this family. There is no barrier to entry. There is no cap on a number. All of us are just sinners in need of a Savior. Let us not stop forgiving and reconciling with one another. We all need to know that there is no sin that excludes us from communing with God and communing with his people. Meeting the king transforms everything.

Saul's community was transformed so that the people he used to hate became the people that he did life with and loved. He experienced God through working through the church to meet his needs but also he experienced God's power to reconcile what Saul had broken. When we meet Jesus our community is transformed. Jesus has the power to restore our broken relationships and he delights in meeting our needs and the needs of others through his people. Meeting the king transforms everything. This brings us to point number three. A corporate transformation. So Saul now welcome into the fold of believers in Jerusalem continues what he's been doing since his conversion. He's preaching the gospel and now he's engaging with a different people group. And that people group is the Hellenist Jews which just means Greek speaking Jews.

Do you remember the last time we ran into the Hellenist Jews? It was back in chapter six. The stoning of Stephen. This is a little window into Luke's brilliance in his writing. The episode that introduced us to Saul was that very episode of Stephen stoning in chapter six and now it's been flipped on its head. Saul used to applaud that. Now he's the one receiving the same persecution that he applauded from the same people only a few years later. The Hellenists continue to reject the gospel and Saul who can't share, who can't stop sharing the good news is forced to flee from Jerusalem to escape yet another plot to kill him. And then our passage closes with this statement in verse 31. So the church throughout all Judea and Galilee and Samaria had peace and was being built up and walking in the fear of the Lord and in the comfort of the Holy Spirit it multiplied.

The church enters a period of peace. Peace. How could that be? The Hellenists are still persecuting Christians. As seen in their plot to kill Saul. He surely wasn't the only one in Jerusalem preaching the gospel. So how could the church have peace when there's such unwavering opposition? How could there be a sense of rest within the community in such a chaotic and hostile environment? This peace the church experiences is not dependent on their current circumstance. The peace they have is a result of their faith in their God.

They have just been eyewitnesses to the continued transforming power of God to save souls including the chief persecutor of the church. God just saved that man. If he can do that he really is for his people. He really is protecting his people. He really is in control. The church grew in peace because they grew in their trust in God. They got to see God at work and saw how good he was towards them. That he was on their side and that he was working at all times to care for him and for them. Oftentimes our peace is contingent on our circumstances. Too often anxiety grips us when there is unresolved conflict, a distressing situation, a lack of clarity for what to do next, or just feeling like life is out of control.

But we are reminded both individually and corporately that our peace comes not from our present circumstances but in our God who is actively working on our behalf for our good. Meeting the king transforms everything. And the church isn't just growing in peace but it's also growing in number. Even in the face of persecution more souls were being added to the family of God and that the church was being transformed in number didn't stop at this point in Acts. For while it was unfortunate for Saul to once again be schemed against, God was using his exit to prepare him for his next mission. His next mission continues even to this day. What is that mission? It's to bring the gospel to the Gentiles. And who's counted among the Gentiles? Well unless you're of Jewish descent in this room, it's you and it's me.

In some way, we are hearing the gospel today because of the result of God's work in saving Saul. Jesus appointed Saul to bring the gospel to the Gentiles and his efforts paved the way, the long winding road that brought us the gospel here 2,000 years later. You see, when the gospel transforms one person, it leads to the transformation of another and another and another and another. We might not be writing holy scripture like Saul was, but make no mistake, believer, that when you become a Christian, you are another light in this dark, broken, and twisted world. The light of the gospel shines forth from you and will continue to do so until our Lord returns. The spread of the gospel is infectious. We may not always see it, but the gospel is always moving. It's always reaching more people, and it happens with one soul saved at a time.

Meeting the king transforms everything. It begins its work in our hearts. It redefines our community, and one person at a time, it transforms our communities. A few application points as we close. If you're here today and you're not a believer, thank you for being here. Your participation in our hour-and-a-half long service is no small sacrifice. We know you could be many places on a Sunday morning. But if you're here, you're here for one reason or another, and you've just heard that Jesus is on a rescue mission, a mission to save souls and to bring them into his family. Maybe as you evaluate your own heart, you see a need for transformation. If that's the case, I can promise you this.

Jesus does not withhold his grace and transforming power from any who would humble themselves and confess their sins to him. If you don't understand what that looks like, I encourage you to talk to a believer. If you don't know a believer, myself or another church leader would be happy to connect with you and share what it means to meet with Jesus. If you're feeling a prick in your heart, don't ignore it. Press in. Ask questions and know that Jesus is ready to answer them. Jesus is ready to meet with you. If you're a believer this morning and you examine your words and see that it reveals a heart of hidden sin or idolatry, be encouraged. God's work does not end at salvation. His transforming work is day by day happening in our hearts.

We are being renewed by the gospel of grace through the power of the Holy Spirit to become degree by degree more like Jesus. Confess your sin to Jesus. Confess your need for a transformed heart and invite fellow believers into your time of need. Jesus will not reject you or leave you to your own devices. And lastly, Christian, remember that God can and will use you both to spread the gospel and meet the needs of his people should you be obedient. Come to church with the expectation of meeting someone new and welcoming them into the fold. Invest time into communing with the saints and welcoming others into that same community. Learn of one another's needs. Be strengthened by the spirit and transforming news that the gospel has with everyone you come in contact with. There is nothing more joyous than seeing the power of the gospel at work in transforming us, transforming our fellow believers, and transforming our communities.

Would you pray with me? Lord, how kind you are to be the initiator. You initiated Saul's conversion on the road to Damascus. and you initiate your work in our hearts. God, I pray that you would be at work in our hearts this morning. God, I pray that where sin needs to be uprooted, that you would meet us in our need. God, I pray that where there's an external need, anywhere within the church, that you would use your church to meet that need. And Lord, I pray that we would continue to grow day by day, degree by degree, into the likeness of Jesus. And Lord, would you give us peace in our soul as we await for you. And we pray these things in Jesus' name. Amen.

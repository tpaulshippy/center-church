Good Friday

Rich Richardson · Mar 30, 2024 · 00:29:46

The good King is there for his people.

Let's pray. Good Friday, our hearts are drawn to a pivotal moment in history.

A moment of profound sacrifice and ultimate love.

But nearly 600 years before the events chronicled in John 19, another scene unfolded. A scene that echoes with the weight of decision and the depths of human brokenness.

Imagine with me, if you will, a king. A king faced with an agonizing choice between his own safety and the welfare of his people.

This king, King Zedekiah, stood at the precipice of destruction as the Babylonian forces relentlessly besieged the city of Jerusalem. And the prophet Jeremiah, a voice of divine guidance in tumultuous times, presented a stark option.

Surrender yourself to the Babylonians and spare your people from their suffering.

What would he do?

Would he selflessly march out the gates, not knowing what would come? Would he hand himself over to be humiliated and made a mockery of while his foes and his countrymen stood by watching him? The thought of this is hard to imagine.

But when the alternative is certain destruction for the many, how could a great man resist the opportunity to save his people? Yet, when the shadows of doom loomed large and the breath of destruction grew even closer, Zedekiah made his choice. A choice that would forever define his legacy. In a moment of staggering consequence, he chose self-preservation. He chose it over sacrificial love. He chose a few more breaths over the many lives in the walls. He chose to abandon his people and give them into the hands of destruction. He chose himself over his people. And honestly, let's be honest with ourselves now, that's exactly what most, if not all kings, would always choose. But tonight, tonight we remember a king, the good king, the best king, that made another choice.

Jesus is the king who gives himself for his people. Let's turn to the gospel.

John 19, starting at the end of verse 16.

So they took Jesus and he went out bearing his own cross to the place called the place of a skull, which in Aramaic is called Golgotha.

And there they crucified him.

And with him two others, one on either side and Jesus between them. Pilate also wrote an inscription and put it on the cross. It read Jesus of Nazareth, king of the Jews. Many of the Jews read this inscription for the place where Jesus was crucified was near the city and it was written in Aramaic, in Latin, and in Greek.

So the chief priest of the Jews said to Pilate, do not write to the king of the Jews, but rather this man said, I am king of the Jews. Pilate answered, what I have written, I have written. And when the soldiers had crucified Jesus, they took his garments and divided them into four parts. One part for each soldier, also his tunic. But the tunic was seamless, woven in one piece from top to bottom. So they said to one another, let us not tear it, but cast lots for it to see whose it shall be. This was to fulfill the scripture which says, they divided my garments among them and for my clothing they cast lots.

And so the soldiers did these things. But standing by the cross of Jesus were his mother and his mother's sister, Mary, the wife of Clopas, and Mary Magdalene.

And when Jesus saw his mother and the disciple whom he loved standing nearby, he said to his mother, woman, behold, your son.

Then he said to his disciple, behold, your mother.

And from that hour the disciple took her to his own home.

There is no question that these short ten verses give us very little explanation of what happened that somber day. to give you an idea of this, I want to point us to the simplicity of what's said in verse 18. He was crucified there. That's it.

That's the crucifixion according to John.

That's all that's given for us to consider by the author. And he spends, if you look, he actually spends more time talking about the place where it happened than the act itself. It's strikingly simple.

Yet, if you are in the company of those who heard these words for the first time, it would absolutely paralyze you with horror.

That is, the crucifixion was so terrible that merely speaking of it ushered the presence of fear. The mention of it was designed to bring paralysis. This is the idea behind something like crucifixion after all. Its greatest effect is not actually carrying it out. It is the fear of it that sits in the minds of those who know it. It's the shame that people want to avoid. the consideration of agony was mechanized to suppress any thought or consideration of rebellion and stomp out all seeds of disloyalty. Pound for pound, these words would knock out the most vicious opposition and usher terror into the minds of anyone in the first century.

So how do we tonight come to a passage like this with something so horrific and leave strengthened and reminded that today is truly good? I believe there is a way.

It is for us to simply look at the good king and see how Jesus does not run from danger, but instead he gives himself for his people. He embodies total sacrifice by owning his duty, caring for the most vulnerable, and securing ultimate victory.

The good king gives himself for his people and he does so like I said by owning his duty. By the time we enter the scene of this passage, Jesus has been through it. From everything I could tell in studying and looking at this in these accounts, Jesus has estimated to have been without sleep for nearly two days. Two days and no rest.

The events of his passion start in the late evening and we know it was late at the time if you recall, Jesus is telling his disciples to do what? Stay awake. And they struggle. So by the time the interrogations come and the beatings start flying and the whips are cracking, Jesus is toast. He is beaten up.

His treatment was unjust and the violence he suffered was absolutely gruesome, but he was exhausted. And after all the beatings, all the scourging, Jesus then is lined up and paraded before the crowds and mocked. Think about this. He is mocked by the very people he spent his life trying to serve.

Imagine that.

Just think about that. Giving yourself to people day in and day out doing everything you know how to help them, teaching them, encouraging them when the world would just cheat them, feeding them when many would send them on their way hungry, walking miles and miles to tend to their dying children, giving patience in exchange for ignorance, returning kindness for those who have no honor, attentively fielding questions at every turn, knowing they are often set as traps.

Imagine all of this being done day after day after day for years just to find that the very crowds that once would pack in to hear him teach were now chanting for his death.

What would you do? What would you do?

I know what I would do.

It wouldn't take much. I know what I would do. I would do anything I could think of to get out of it. I would. And like Zedekiah, I would be thinking about one thing and one thing only. How to make it stop. Make no mistake, friends. If we're faced with this, there would not be this like little somber moment of hesitation. No moment of pause. When we get to that crossroads, we take the quickest line to safety and we do it over and over again.

We would lie, we would argue, we would beg, and we would plead. We would sell each other out and we would look for any ray of hope to escape what we knew was coming. That's because I would not be a good king and neither would you.

But we are not talking about us. We're talking about Jesus. He is a good king and the good king owns his duty.

We find this king who does not turn from his mission. Who does not flinch at every strike. He does not sell out his people when they mock him. He picks up his cross and as the text says, he picks up his own cross and he carries it. He calls it his own and he marches towards destruction. In the face of terror, he refuses to forsake his duty.

He gives himself to his people.

The good king gives himself for his people also by caring for the most vulnerable. If you look at verse 25, it gives us a picture of what I think is the most incredible picture of love and care that I personally have ever seen. It says that next to where Jesus was being crucified was his mother, two other women, and his disciple, his friend. Now if you're not aware, the process of crucifixion is designed to get in your head.

It's designed to be an awful experience not just for the one who is suffering it, but for those who see it with their own eyes.

And so when we enter the scene, it is beyond words to express the kind of sadness and suffering and torment being experienced by Jesus, but certainly and absolutely for those who are with him. There is no greater fear for a parent than to see their children in pain.

Many of you have come into this room tonight with new children. And you know this already. There is no greater torment than to think about your children being in pain. There is nothing that will rob your sleep more quickly than the fear of trouble for those we spend our lives raising and caring for. Nothing is more torturous than a parent wanting to do something and not being able to help.

Yet in this moment, this moment of deepest sadness out of the fatigue, out of the pain, out of the noise, the good king sees his mother in anguish and he cares for her.

The good king does not neglect the fears of his people. He doesn't push them off. He gives himself and he cares for the most vulnerable. Mary, his mother, was the most vulnerable kind of person in this society. For her son to die awful enough as it is, she would not only bear the loss but she would bear the loss and it would continue day after day in shame.

Your son was who?

That guy they just killed?

We know that she had other children. We certainly do. But guess who is not mentioned at the cross with her? Those other children.

So in a moment of great care, Jesus, the good king, barely able to hold himself up, does not dismiss her pain. But he sympathetically cares for her. And looking at his friend, he tells her, take her as his own mother. Take care for her as Jesus would himself. He cares for her, yes. And if you think about all of the ruckus and all of the chaos and the screaming and the pleading and the mockery, everything that's going on, what happens for Mary and those who care about Jesus? It's something amazing.

This kind love that he extends to her quiets all of the noise.

And in the chilling horror of this awful day, it provides a kind of warmth for those who would otherwise just simply be forgotten. The good king gives himself for his people. As we take a moment to pause and think, I want us to consider the love of God and Christ. The kind of compassion we are dealing with here in these words. Even as parents, we are so quick, you may not be like me, but I'll be focused and in something, and my kids will come running in, and they're fighting or something, and some kind of chaos is breaking loose, and I ask to shush them, I ask them to pause. Certainly nobody else in here has ever experienced that, I'm sure, but this is the kind of thing that we do when we're focused or dedicated to a cause, right? Or maybe if you haven't had that experience, you've been in the middle of something and somebody's calling you, you just put mute for a second, you finish what you're doing, and then you get to them, right?

Think about this.

Here, though, we see the kind of love and compassion that transcends all models. All examples. It is the kind of love that does not put people on hold, but bears their burdens as if there's nothing else going on in the world at that moment. Never too busy.

Never too tired.

Never impatient, just constantly giving himself all the time, even when he is beaten to a pulp.

What else would we expect, though?

And all of the drama of this account, the motion of the religious leaders, the mob and Pilate is one that believes Jesus is being subdued, that he is being beaten down, that he is being put in his place. All the while in their plotting and scheming, there is something more glorious going on. In the backdrop of this great humiliation is the eternal work of the triune God set in motion. The focus of Christ's hand through all of the beatings and the scourging and the humiliation is set on one thing and one thing alone. The salvation of his people. The mob the Roman soldiers, spineless pilots, the religious mafia, with all of their plans, are nothing more than cardboard backdrops and fading lights compared to what's really going on here.

The good king is at work. He is giving himself for his people as the perfect spotless sacrifice for sin.

He believed the scripture.

He gives himself as a spotless lamb. As a perfect atoning sacrifice. He carries his own cross up the hill because he and only he can fully pay for the sins of the world. For his people. For you and I. He does this in the most magnificent way. He takes all the wickedness meant for him.

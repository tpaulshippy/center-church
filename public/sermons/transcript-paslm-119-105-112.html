We need a trustworthy guide, we need the word of God to be our Guide.

Is it my turn? Okay, let's do it. Yeah. All right.

Anytime my wife and I are driving somewhere for the first or second time, something strange happens. I feel like I instantly become like the great renowned world explorer Ferdinand Magellan, an expert navigator on the road who can guide us to our destination using the stars or the streets and the valley that are laid out for us like a grid or perhaps the lights on South Mountain. Or so I think. Really, whenever we head somewhere for the first time, we're usually in for some sort of conflict. And it's because I overestimate my sense of direction. Because as soon as we leave our neighborhood, I'm faced with this perilous decision. Do I swallow my pride and ask my lovely bride to open up Apple or Google Maps and guide us to our destination? Or do I double down and just try to get us there without the use of any help?

Well, I can tell you that the moment of sanctification comes as we're driving and as we're approaching maybe the first, second, third turn, even out of our neighborhood. At about the 500 foot mark, I opt for asking my wife which way we should go. Thank God for GPS. Thank God for my lovely wife because I am certainly no Magellan. And I can't tell you how many times I've had to ask my wife to open Google or Apple Maps just so we can get to where we're going on time. Now I say all of this because to get to a destination for the first or even second, third, fourth time, we need trustworthy guides. Now something frustrating about GPS, and it doesn't happen as much as it used to, is when you plug in the destination and it takes you somewhere different because a road or an address hasn't been updated in the app.

Or it doesn't show you that the 60 is shut down due to construction, or maybe a road is closed because there's a marathon or something. What that does is it leaves us leaving feeling a little bit misguided, even led astray.

But let me tell you this, what is even more devastating than faulty GPS is giving in to the many false guides that try to lead us astray in our daily lives. We can be tempted to follow any one of the following. This is not an exhaustive list, but we can be tempted to follow the false guide of pop psychology, which tempts us into thinking that every unpleasant experience of our past is tantamount to trauma. And we need to dive headlong into this trauma to best understand who we are today. Or how about the false guide of expressive individualism? Expressive individualism sings the siren song that we're not our truest selves unless we're following our heart and satisfying our basis desires, thus relativizing and bending and distorting the truth to fit our own perceived needs. Or how about the false guide of politics? Which tries to lure us in with its empty promises of a utopian society where everyone shares the same view as you on every social issue?

And it has this unrealistic expectation of a world that's free from pain and suffering. Or how about the false guide of autonomy, which tries to convince us that living an isolated life, a life that's independent of help and guidance from others, is what is truly best for us.

Now, like someone who finds themselves lost due to a faulty or an outdated GPS system, we can get lost in a world that's teeming with these ideologies, with these ideas that oppose the truth of God's word. But as Christians, we cannot allow ourselves to follow false guides. You and I, we need a trustworthy guide. We need the word of God to guide us. Today's passage, much like Psalm 119, has shown us that the word of God is our guide.

It would be easy from this point on to propose to you some pithy main idea like God's word's a guide. It's true, God's word is a guide, but it's a bit vague. So, to provide some clarity on exactly how the word's a guide, I want us to ask the question, it's a rhetorical question. What does the word of God guide us to?

Does it lead us toward having better and more refined morals? Does it guide us toward a slavish devotion to God? Does it give us insight into what career to choose or which spouse to marry or how to live a better, more successful life? Or does it do something altogether different?

Now, I want to propose to you three different ways, these will be our points for this morning, that I believe the word guides us. First, the word guides us in knowing God.

Secondly, the word guides us through suffering. And thirdly, our last point will be the word guides us toward joyful obedience. I believe these three points serve to reinforce one main idea that we get from our passage, and it's this: God uses his word to draw us near to himself. God uses his word to draw us near to himself. If you have a Bible or a device that you read your Bible on, please turn with me to Psalm 119.

Psalm 119 is saturated with this language that conveys the psalmist's love for the law. It also possesses language that conveys the psalmist's desire to keep God's law. In our stanza, the psalmist writes how he has sworn an oath and confirmed it to keep God's righteous rules. Now this comes immediately after a verse you're probably well familiar with, and that is verse 105, where he writes, "Your word is a lamp to my feet and a light to my path." Now the psalmist uses words like lamp and light for a reason, and that's to show us that the word of God is a trustworthy guide. We need light to see, and the word provides us with that light, and it helps us to know God and his righteous rules better. Well, the psalmist loves God and his law so much that he's determined to keep God's law.

It's entirely appropriate for you and I to consider to ask ourselves the question, "Does God's law have any bearing on my life?" Like the psalmist, do I need to keep God's righteous rules?" Now the easy way out of this conundrum is the classic children's ministry answer to any question, and that is Jesus. Yes, Jesus has fulfilled the law. You would be right if you came up with that answer. And if you're in Christ, you share in his perfect righteousness and obedience. We read of how Jesus fulfilled the law in Matthew 5:17, where it's written, "Do not think that I have come to abolish," which means destroy or dismantle the law or the prophets. "I have not come to abolish them, but to fulfill or to bring them into fruition," Jesus says. Jesus didn't come to merely fulfill Old Testament predictions regarding himself, things that we celebrate on Easter and Christmas.

Isaiah 9, Isaiah 53, passages like those. Jesus says he came to fulfill every part of the Old Testament. How does that work? Let me explain. Let's use the example of the Levitical priesthood and the sacrificial system that God commanded the Israelites to use. All of the ceremonial laws and sacrifices that you would find in reading Leviticus were meant to foreshadow a greater reality. They were meant to point us forward toward our need for a final sacrifice that would fully atone for sin, one that required blood to be spilled, one that required death.

The reason priests in the Old Testament had to continually kill goats, pigeons, lambs, and bulls, it was a bloody mess. If we were there, we would retch from the stench.

The reason why they did this is because these temporal sacrifices pointed toward the need for an eternal sacrifice, one that was accomplished through Jesus offering his life as a perfect atonement for sin.

Now, the law, as outlined in the first five books of the Bible, also pointed to the need for a priest. So we talked about sacrifice, but the law also pointed toward a need for a priest. Someone who was called by God, who would take the ceremonies and sacrifices seriously, and who would consecrate themselves in perfect holiness to obey all that God commands.

As Christian readers, we know that Jesus was that high priest. He alone could obey each law, and he alone consecrated himself in perfect holiness.

In him, in Christ, we have a perfect sacrifice and a perfect priest. A two-for-one, if you will.

Now, this begs the question for us. If we're being astute readers of the Bible, we'd find ourselves asking this question. If there were no perfect priests back then, if there were no perfect sacrifices, how could the psalmist say that he can keep God's law?

And, what's more, is the psalmist was on the other side of redemptive history. He was writing this before Jesus came to accomplish the work of salvation. So what gives? How could the psalmist say something like this?

It was because the psalmist believed by faith in the promise of the perfect sacrifice to come. He believed that this perfect priest would come and offer a perfect sacrifice. He looked forward to the day when the promised great high priest from the line of Melchizedek would come and offer himself as the perfect sacrifice, and sit down at the right hand of God. If you want to read more about that, read the book of Hebrews.

My friends, the psalmist anticipated and believed in the gospel.

And so he has every right to say he can keep God's righteous rules, because he had the Spirit of God dwelling within him, which causes him to keep God's law. But back to our original question, does God's law have any bearing on you? Does it have any bearing on me?

If you believe the gospel, you, like the psalmist, can keep God's law, and you should strive to uphold it.

And what's more, every person who believes the promise of the gospel, and who has the Spirit of God, is a priest.

Now, you might be wondering, how so? Well, the psalmist understood this. He would have known the words God spoke to Moses from Exodus 19.6, when the Lord said, And you, Israel, shall be to me a kingdom of priests, and a holy nation.

The same is true of us. 1 Peter 2.5 says, We are a holy priesthood, intended to offer spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God through Jesus Christ. We're empowered for service unto God by the sacrificial death of Jesus Christ. And now, we serve as God's priests.

Paul draws on this language in Romans 12, When he tells them to present their bodies as living sacrifices, holy, acceptable, pleasing to the Lord. The psalmist even offers a free will offering to the Lord in verse 108, which signifies his acceptance of his role as a priest unto God. Why does this matter? Sounds like a lot of information. Why does it matter?

Because as God's priests, we're held to a high standard, like the Levitical priests were. You and I are called to be holy because God is holy.

Now, there are benefits to being a priest. Think about it. We're intended to be near to God. Priests had the privilege of being able to spend time in the temple and in the tabernacle. They were intended to spend time near to God's presence.

He knows your frame. He knows what you struggle with. He knows that you're weak. But he invites us to draw near.

We learn about what he delights in, what he takes pleasure in.

This is how, as God's priests, we need to approach our relationship with God. We need to spend time with him in his word, grow in our affections and our love for him, and draw near to him by his grace.

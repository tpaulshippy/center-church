Summer in the Psalms- Psalm 119: 81-88

Rich Richardson · Jul 21, 2024

God's word is far more valuable than anything else

Thanks, Frank.

Alright, as Frank said, we'll be in Psalm 119 again, so if you would open your Bibles, I'll be reading from verse 81. That's where we're going to start today. Before we do, I'd like to pray briefly.

Father God, may you be with us today. May the meditations of our heart and the longings of our soul be towards you and open our ears to hear your word.

Let my voice recede, let your voice come to the forefront and build hope and faith in your people. In Christ's name, amen. Have you ever driven through the fog?

You know, the kind of fog where it's so thick you can barely see the taillights of the car in front of you?

Or better yet, have you ever been caught in a blinding snowstorm? The kind where you grip your steering wheel in terror as you lose sight of not only the other cars, but the very icy road upon which you travel.

Now, I realize here in Arizona, fog banks and snow blizzards are not the norm. But there is one driving hazard in the desert that rivals that of fog or blizzard.

It's the mighty dust storm, or as meteorologists call it, the haboob.

Now, a haboob is not a mild-mannered cloud of dust.

It can be a mile high of high-speed dirt that envelops you, suffocates you, and reduces your visibility to near zero.

I remember driving in my first dust storm shortly after moving to Arizona many years ago.

When I first caught sight of that big wall of dust, I was fascinated. I was strangely drawn to it. Instead of pulling off the road like every other sensible driver, I soldiered on, wondering what it would be like to drive through a haboob.

But upon entering, my sense of wonder quickly changed to desperation. I couldn't see anything.

I couldn't see the road. I couldn't see the oncoming traffic. I couldn't even make out the front of my car's hood.

But foolishly, I drove on.

I was frightened, but I also was confident in my ability to drive straight and see my car safely to the other side.

And sure enough, I did make it to the other side. And somehow, I managed not to run into anything except for a tumbleweed or two that rolled across my windshield. When the dust finally settled, when the dust finally settled, I was stunned to realize where I was.

My car had somehow meandered across the oncoming lanes of traffic, off the far left shoulder, and onto a dry, rocky riverbed. I had survived, but that desperate drive through the haboob had not gone as planned. The confidence I placed in my own ability to navigate through zero visibility proved to be reckless.

It was by the hand of God alone that my car didn't crash that day and that I stand here today to tell about it. And that's a lesson I could have learned from today's text. For as we shall see, the author of today's text found himself in desperate, zero visibility circumstances too. But rather than trust his own instincts to see himself through, he placed confidence in someone else. In one who could truly help him. In one who would surely give him life and enable him to live to tell of it.

The calf stanza of Psalm 119. And yes, if you look in your Bibles, you'll see a little heading above it. K-A-P-H. That's the Hebrew letter that heads every one of these verses. Every one of these verses in Hebrew begins with this letter calf, or letter K in our language. This calf stanza is the darkest set of verses in this psalm.

But while verses 84, 85, 86, and 87 speak of obvious trouble from without, like the persecution in the pits, this desperation is actually also in the earlier verses of our stanza. But they're hidden a bit. Verses 81 and 82 point to a subtle trouble within.

The first two verses of the calf stanza begin with a verb that our English Bibles translate to long for. You see it there.

My soul longs. My eyes long. But in the original, the word is more forlorn.

It carries with it the idea of to be at an end.

To be spent.

My soul is spent waiting for your salvation. Verse 81. My eyes fail looking for your promise. Verse 82.

In verse 81, the psalmist isn't just longing for God's salvation. His very soul is exhausted with waiting for rescue. He doesn't know when God will show up or how his rescue might come about. His soul is spent. And in verse 82, he isn't just patiently waiting for God's promise. His eyes can't see from where it may come. His vision is obscured. It's as if he's in the midst of a dust storm. The eyes of his heart are blinded.

And then verse 83 paints a similar picture as the psalmist opines. I've become like a wineskin in the smoke.

He feels as if he's enveloped in a cloud of smoke. He can hardly breathe, let alone see what's going on. And this word, smoke, suggests something more sinister.

It hints at the cataclysmic destruction of the place where he dwells, his home.

This word doesn't occur very often in our Bibles, but it occurs twice in one particular verse in Genesis chapter 19, talking about the story of God's destruction of two cities with fire and brimstone.

In Genesis 19.28, it says this, And Abraham looked down towards Sodom and Gomorrah and toward all the land of the valley, and he looked, and behold, the smoke of the land went up like the smoke of a furnace.

Like Sodom and Gomorrah, our psalmist feels as if everything he calls home here on earth has gone up in smoke.

No wonder he cries out at the end of verse 86, Help me!

What a desperate cry from a man in an unspeakably desperate situation.

He feels abandoned, left for dead, in a burned out, smoking ruin, and he doesn't know when it will end.

But curiously, this desperate man hasn't lost all hope. He may not know when or how he will be rescued, but he does know who will bring it about.

Notice his confidence in these very same verses.

It all starts in that first one, verse 81.

My soul may be spent as it longs for your salvation, but I still hope in your word despite the magnitude of his trials, despite the intensity of his affliction, he is not without hope. Rather, he is full of hope.

It isn't a trust in his own abilities. It isn't relying upon his own experience to navigate through the dust storm of his desperate situation. No. He has placed his hope somewhere else. As the text says, I hope in your word. Whose word? The word of the Lord.

Our psalmist placed his hope entirely in what God's word proclaims and promises.

And this hope isn't an unsure, doubtful hope, a wishful, thinking hope, a hope that doesn't really believe anything's going to come good to this. No. It's a certain hope. A sure hope. A confident hope. Because biblical hope is reliable. Because it springs from the very character and the very certitude of God himself. because our God is sure, his word is sure, and our hope in his word is sure. And the psalmist knows this.

That's why his question to God in the very next verse, verse 82, isn't, will you comfort me?

No.

It isn't a matter of if, but when. Because our psalmist knows his God from texts like Isaiah 51, 12, where the Lord says, I, I am he who comforts you. Who are you that you are afraid of man who dies or the son of man who is made like grass?

He's also aware of a psalm that we're all aware of and that Olivia referred to this morning. Psalm 23, verse 4.

Even though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil. for you are with me. Your rod and your staff, they comfort me.

Though his eyes may fail now as they look for the promise of the Lord, our promise remains confident that God most certainly will comfort him in due time. and as he goes on to lament the many persecutions and pitfalls that the insolent have dug for him in verses 84 through 87, even then his confidence in God's word does not wane.

In verse 84, he knows those trafficking and persecution are setting themselves up for a fall and it's not a matter of if but when they will be judged.

In verse 86, while enduring an onslaught of lies, he boldly declares that all your commandments are sure, which is another way of saying they're faithful and true. in so doing, he stakes his claim that the faithful and sure word of God will prevail over the lies and falsehood of his enemies.

Kind of like Isaiah 25 declares. In verse 1, it says, O Lord, you're my God. I will exalt you. I will praise your name for you have done wonderful things, plans formed from of old, faithful and sure. And then later in verse 5 of the same chapter concerning the insolent ones, God, you subdue the noise of the foreigners as heat by the shade of a cloud so the songs of the ruthless are put down.

You see, throughout his desperation, our psalmist remains confident in the faithfulness of another.

His cry for help at the end of verse 86 may seem desperate, but he knows his help will surely come.

But how?

How can he be so confident in the word of the Lord? What makes him think that all those promises in the Bible are addressed to him and not to someone else? What guarantees that certainty?

Well, verse 88, the one we haven't looked at yet, gives us the underlying reason for this confidence.

His confidence has everything to do with that first phrase in verse 88.

In your steadfast love.

You see, the psalmist finds himself in God's steadfast love.

Now, steadfast love is a very common phrase in the psalms. The author of Psalm 119 revels in it no less than seven times. But what does it mean?

One way to figure out its meaning is to review how it's translated in other versions, because believe it or not, just about every version translates it differently. We're using ESV here.

In the original Greek, it was translated simply as mercy.

The King James and the New American Standard call it loving kindness.

The NIV uses unfailing love.

And the Christian Standard Bible calls it faithful love.

Now, if you put all these concepts together, you get something along the lines of a steady unfailing love coupled with an overwhelming kindness and mercy.

It's covenantal love and a love that doesn't go away.

And it's something that God bestows freely upon undeserving subjects.

For according to Scripture, we're undeserving because we all once lived in the passions of our flesh, carrying out the desires of the body and mind, and we're by nature children of wrath, the wrath of God, together with the rest of mankind.

But wonder of wonders, God chose to love us anyway.

Not because we deserved it, but because of his remarkable steadfast love.

But how? How did he love us so steadfastly, so faithfully, so unfailingly? through his son Jesus, that's how.

As this passage from 1 John clearly states, 1 John 4, 9, and 10, in this, the love of God was made manifest among us, that God sent his only son into the world, so that we might live through him.

That's one reason. And the second verse is like it, but slightly different.

Verse 10, in this is love, not that we have loved God, but that he loved us and sent his son, Jesus, to be the propitiation sin.

That means wrath bearer, the one who took care of the wrath.

The propitiation for our sins. Notice how when we know God loves us, first, we know he loves us because he sent his son to earth to bear the wrath we deserved for our many sins by dying on the cross. So we know God loves us because of what Jesus did when he died on the cross. And the other verse, we also know he loves us because he made us alive together with Christ when Jesus rose from the dead three days later.

That's how we know. That's how God's steadfast love is revealed to us. He sent his son to die in our stead and then he raised him from the dead and raised everyone else who's with him. With him.

The reason our psalmist can endure all the affliction and persecution and pits of despair with confident hope is because he knows and trusts in the certainty of God's steadfast love.

And this, this truth may be the key to understanding the most elusive metaphor in these eight verses.

For no one really uses them anymore. Just think of them as hollowed out animal skin sewed together and used to store and transport wine for foot soldiers or weary travelers. They were common back in Bible times, but today we typically store our wine, obviously, in long neck bottles made of glass. But in verse 83, our psalmist compares himself to a wineskin suffocating in the smoke.

But fresh wineskins can and do serve a purpose. They hold wine, and they keep it safe and secure, and then they dispense it to others as needed. perhaps our weary psalmist saw himself that way, as a keeper and a dispenser of wine to the weary, weary ones like us.

He was standing on God's commandments and living according to God's law and fearing God's judgments and not forgetting God's statutes and longing for God's promise while ever fixing his hope on God's word. So what of us, what can we learn from Psalm 119, verses 81 through 88?
